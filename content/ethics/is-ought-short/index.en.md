# The Solution to the Is-Ought Problem: A Detailed Outline Based on Popper and Bunge

## Introduction

Ever since David Hume observed in his *Treatise of Human Nature*, published in 1739–1740, that philosophers first discuss what is and then abruptly shift to what ought to be, a fundamental question has remained: How can this transition be justified? Hume stated that he could not see how this new relation could follow from the preceding statements and demanded an explanation (Hume, 1739–1740/1978, Book 3, Part 1, Section 1).

The is-ought problem determines whether an objective ethical standard can be derived from reality without additionally presupposing it as a moral prescription.

To date, however, there has been no solution to this problem. Many philosophers even consider it insoluble or regard the impossibility of such a transition as proven. Previous attempts at proof, however, have been restricted to particular systems and must necessarily remain restricted to particular semantic and logical systems. They therefore establish only that these systems are unsuitable for solving the problem ([Delor, 2026](https://philpapers.org/rec/DELTST), Section 4). A system in which such a transition is impossible no longer says anything about reality because it cannot fail against reality ([Delor, 2026](https://philpapers.org/rec/DELTST), Sections 3.2.6–3.2.7 and 3.3).

In this post, I would like to outline the solution to this problem that I develop extensively and in detail, including potential objections, in my paper *The Solution to the Is-Ought Problem Based on Critical Rationalism and Evolutionary Theory* ([Delor, 2026](https://philpapers.org/rec/DELTST)).

## Epistemological and Ontological Foundations

Ethics concerns real beings, their decisions, and the consequences of their actions. Its claims must therefore be testable against reality. The best ontological foundation available to us for this purpose is Bunge’s materialist ontology ([Delor, 2026](https://philpapers.org/rec/DELTST), Sections 3 and 3.1): The world consists of real things and processes that are lawfully interconnected and interact with one another (Bunge, 1977, 1979).

Popper (1934/1959) rightly observed that a claim that cannot fail against reality says nothing about reality. The world is not simply everything that is the case, since that would be circular, but everything that is lawfully interconnected or interacts with other things (Bunge, 1977, 1979). Consequently, nothing real is beyond every conceivable form of testing, at least in principle. A statement that remains untestable against reality in principle, even under the most extraordinary conditions, no longer says anything about reality and must therefore be rejected ([Delor, 2026](https://philpapers.org/rec/DELTST), Sections 3.1 and 3.2.6–3.2.7).

## The Ontology of Values

In the history of philosophy and in linguistic usage, we find two fundamentally different ways of characterizing values: categorical, one-place concepts of value and relational concepts of value ([Delor, 2026](https://philpapers.org/rec/DELTST), Sections 5.2–5.3). The former attribute value to something in itself, independently of a subject, its goals, and the relevant conditions. Relational concepts of value, by contrast, identify the value of something for a particular subject in achieving a goal under given conditions.

The categorical concept of value fails because no possible experience can show whether something is actually “good in itself.” According to the criterion of testability established by critical rationalism (Popper, 1934/1959), as explained above, it therefore lacks a testable connection to reality. The same applies to a categorical ought whose validity is supposed to be independent of all real relations to goals ([Delor, 2026](https://philpapers.org/rec/DELTST), Sections 5.2 and 6.1).

This leaves the relational concept of value, following Bunge’s theory of value (1989): **Something is valuable to a subject insofar as it promotes that subject’s goal under the given conditions.** Whether this relation of promotion exists is a testable factual question. Dependence on a subject and a context therefore does not imply subjective arbitrariness.

It follows that, among the concepts of ought found in actual linguistic usage, only a goal-relative ought can be relevant to ethics. On this basis, I formulate the ophelic operator more precisely: An action ought to be performed if and only if, under the given conditions, it promotes the achievement of a subject’s goal better than all other known solutions and is expected to minimize the risk of ruin. Naturally, the uncertainty of our knowledge and predictions must also be taken into account ([Delor, 2026](https://philpapers.org/rec/DELTST), Section 6.1).

## Outline of the Reconstruction

On this basis, the solution can now be reconstructed. The following steps are derived and justified in greater detail in my paper ([Delor, 2026](https://philpapers.org/rec/DELTST), Sections 6.4–6.9.2; section references follow German version 0.9.9.9).

1. **Ought is goal-relative.** As shown in the preceding section, ought can only be reconstructed as a relation between an action, a goal, and the given conditions that is testable against reality.

2. **Goals are hierarchically ordered.** Instrumental goals are pursued to achieve higher-order goals. An ultimate goal, by contrast, serves no further goal. This distinction can already be found in Aristotle (NE 1.7, 1097a25–1097b21).

3. **We cannot freely choose ultimate goals.** We can rationally select instrumental goals by reference to higher-order standards. Choosing those standards would, in turn, presuppose a standard. Libertarian free will does not solve this problem: A decision is either determined by causes or random; randomness establishes neither freedom nor rationality. I explain why the ultimate goal cannot be freely chosen in Section 6.5 of my paper ([Delor, 2026](https://philpapers.org/rec/DELTST)).

4. **The goal structure that actually exists must be investigated empirically.** Whether the hierarchy consists of a single ultimate goal, several independent ultimate goals, or an infinite regress cannot be decided by logic alone. Ultimate goals can neither be stipulated by definition nor identified merely by reflecting on concepts. Their reconstruction must agree with and explain the empirical facts ([Delor, 2026](https://philpapers.org/rec/DELTST), Sections 6.4 and 6.6).

5. **Goals do not require a goal setter.** Conscious purpose-setting must be distinguished from naturally evolved goal-directedness. Pittendrigh (1958) and Mayr (1974) call the latter teleonomy. Random variation and nonrandom selection can produce structures functionally directed toward goals. Dawkins (1986) explains how cumulative selection produces such structures without foresight or planning. Evolution itself pursues no goal.

6. **The goal structures of living beings have evolved.** The same applies to their physical structures, dispositions, instincts, and behavioral mechanisms. Our desires, social behavior, and ability to reflect on our actions likewise rest on evolved capacities. Social rules and the mechanisms of cultural learning have also evolved and can be explained in evolutionary terms; they, too, serve the lineage fitness of the members of the respective group (Henrich, 2016; [Delor, 2026](https://philpapers.org/rec/DELTST), Section 6.9.1). An ultimate goal of living systems must therefore also be reconstructible in evolutionary terms ([Delor, 2026](https://philpapers.org/rec/DELTST), Section 6.7).

7. **The evolved ultimate goal: Lineage fitness.** According to Darwin (1859), heritable differences under conditions of limited resources produce differences in reproductive success. Dawkins (1976/2016) sharpens this account from a gene-centered perspective: Genetic differences manifest themselves in the phenotype, that is, the physical and behavioral characteristics of an organism. Lineages whose characteristics promote their continuation prevail more frequently over competing variants; lineages that permanently fail to continue become extinct. From this, I reconstruct lineage fitness as the intrinsic, teleonomic ultimate goal of living systems ([Delor, 2026](https://philpapers.org/rec/DELTST), Section 6.9): **the long-term persistence, reproduction, and adaptive success of one’s own genealogical lineage under changing environmental conditions.**

This ultimate goal need not be consciously pursued. Hunger, sexual motivation, attachment, and caregiving operate as proximate mechanisms, that is, immediate drives. Individual actions that run counter to the ultimate goal can be explained by harmful changes, malfunctions, or evolutionary mismatch: Mechanisms that promoted lineage fitness under earlier conditions can become misdirected under changed conditions. Nor does blind egoism follow. Assistance to relatives, whose evolutionary basis Hamilton (1964) explains, as well as reciprocal assistance and cooperation, can promote lineage fitness. A detailed explanation of these relationships and a discussion of the corresponding objections can be found in my paper ([Delor, 2026](https://philpapers.org/rec/DELTST), Sections 6.9–6.9.2).

## Result

The ultimate standard has thus been reconstructed from empirical theories without smuggling in a normative standard or a normative premise. Normativity follows solely from the ophelic operator, itself reconstructed from actual linguistic usage and reality, together with the empirically identified ultimate goal to which all instrumental goals are subordinate.

For the first time, this standard allows ethical questions to be answered objectively and ought-statements to be derived from purely factual statements: If, under the given conditions, an action A promotes the ultimate goal of a subject B better than the other known courses of action, taking uncertainty and the risk of ruin into account, then B ought to perform that action.

Both the reconstruction of the standard and its application remain testable against reality and falsifiable. The detailed derivation and discussion of possible objections can be found in my paper ([Delor, 2026](https://philpapers.org/rec/DELTST), Sections 6.11–6.13 and 9).

## Further Objections

Historically, further arguments have been raised against a naturalistic reconstruction of ethics, particularly the naturalistic fallacy, the open-question argument, and, more recently, evolutionary debunking. These are examined in greater detail in the paper ([Delor, 2026](https://philpapers.org/rec/DELTST), Sections 5.1, 7.1, and 8). Many other objections are also addressed, including the charge of *changing the subject*: that the reconstruction describes merely instrumental rather than ethical norms and is therefore morally irrelevant ([Delor, 2026](https://philpapers.org/rec/DELTST), Section 6.2).

## Concluding Remarks

For the first time in the history of philosophy, this account presents a solution to the is-ought problem in which normativity is not smuggled in through additional normative premises or assumptions built into the system. Instead, the operator from which normativity arises is derived from the facts themselves. Both the derivation and reconstruction and the resulting value judgments meet the most advanced standards in the philosophy of science, particularly those of critical rationalism: Nothing is shielded from criticism, and both the derivation and the judgments themselves can be tested against reality.

This implies neither centralized control nor an ethics governed by experts, but the opposite. Everyone is responsible for their own actions and is best placed to make the corresponding decisions for themselves in a decentralized manner. As Hayek (1945) shows, knowledge is dispersed and always incomplete. To make the best possible decisions, those who make them must themselves be exposed to both their negative and their positive consequences. Taleb (2018) calls this *Skin in the Game*.

Nor does this solution bring ethics to an end. The opposite is the case: It first establishes the possibility of an objective ethics. Investigating its consequences and continually reassessing them under the given conditions and in light of new knowledge is an ongoing project that can never be completed. This remains a task for every individual, as well as for philosophers and scientists collectively.
